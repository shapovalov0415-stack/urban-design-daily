## Designing for Country: Planning treats habitat loss as the cost of progress. That is a choice Australia can change

**Source:** The Fifth Estate  
**URL:** https://thefifthestate.com.au/columns/spinifex/designing-for-country-planning-treats-habitat-loss-as-the-cost-of-progress-that-is-a-choice-australia-can-change/  
**Topics:** Australia, Environmental Planning, Urban Ecology, Landscape Urbanism, Indigenous Design

Australia's new National Environment Protection Agency begins operating on 1 July 2026, marking the centrepiece of the largest overhaul of Australian environmental law in more than 25 years. The reformed framework promises faster assessment pathways, more efficient offset markets, and quicker development approvals. Yet this article argues the reform leaves untouched a foundational assumption embedded in how Australian cities are planned and built: that development and biodiversity must compete, and that the planning system exists to broker an acceptable balance between them. That assumption, the piece contends, is so deeply normalised that it is rarely questioned. Rather than asking how to better manage the trade-off, practitioners should ask why we accept it as inevitable. The article draws on First Nations philosophy to propose an alternative frame. Designing for Country is presented not as a symbolic or nostalgic gesture but as a practical reorientation of planning culture, one in which ecological presence becomes a design objective rather than a regulatory constraint to be offset. In concrete terms this means rethinking not only where buildings go and how green space is configured, but how development relates to the living systems it displaces. The goal is to create environments that make the presence of other species visible, legible, and valued within the urban fabric. The timing of the argument is deliberate. As the new agency takes effect, the piece challenges the profession to consider whether streamlined approvals and efficient offset mechanisms will change ecological outcomes, or simply accelerate the same extractive logic under more efficient administrative machinery.

**Why it matters:** The launch of Australia's reformed environmental framework on 1 July 2026 coincides with this pointed critique of the offset-and-approve model underpinning it. For state planning agencies embedding biodiversity provisions into precinct-scale plans, the Designing for Country frame offers a test: whether ecological presence is treated as a design driver or merely a compliance input.

## Julianne's Garden: Reimagining the Space Between Public and Private

**Source:** Common Edge  
**URL:** https://commonedge.org/juliannes-garden-reimagining-the-space-between-public-and-private-life/  
**Topics:** United States, Landscape Design, Public Realm, Placemaking

In Oakland's Rockridge neighbourhood, a front yard garden created by resident Julianne Sherback has become an informal case study in how private landscape can activate public life. Documented by Common Edge, the garden blurs the conventional boundary between domestic space and streetscape through dense planting, found objects, colourful statues, and ceramic fragments arranged over decades of incremental care. The article frames the garden through the concept of rasquache, a DIY aesthetic rooted in Latino cultural tradition that values improvisation, resourcefulness, and personal expression over formal design standards. Rather than conforming to the manicured front lawn typical of American suburban codes, the garden transforms the setback zone into a space of social interaction and sensory richness, inviting neighbours and passersby to slow down and engage. A parallel is drawn to Simon Rodia's Watts Towers in Los Angeles, constructed over 33 years from recycled and found materials including broken porcelain, glass bottles, and seashells. Like the towers, the garden emerged without a professional plan, shaped instead by daily acts of care, experimentation, and accumulated memory. Plants sit at the centre of the composition, some native and others collected from distant places, reflecting the migrations and cultural journeys of the family itself. The piece argues that such gardens challenge conventional landscape metrics centred on property values, order, and visual uniformity. They demonstrate how the threshold between public and private space can function as a generous, community-facing landscape rather than a boundary enforced by turf and fencing. The backyard extends this logic further, creating an immersive environment where afternoon light shines through translucent leaves like stained glass.

**Why it matters:** Front yard landscapes that prioritise personal expression and community engagement over uniform presentation offer a low-cost model for activating the public-private threshold. For Australian medium-density precincts where setback zones often default to hard paving or token planting, the rasquache approach demonstrates how incremental resident-led landscape can generate street-level social life without institutional design investment.

## Some Things YIMBYs Tend To Get Wrong (and Right)

**Source:** Strong Towns  
**URL:** https://www.strongtowns.org/journal/2026-6-30-some-things-yimbys-tend-to-get-wrong-and-right  
**Topics:** United States, Housing Supply, Zoning Reform, Urban Policy

Strong Towns contributor Jonathan Hopkins examines the assumptions underlying the YIMBY movement's central claim that building more market-rate housing will broadly improve affordability. The article acknowledges a growing body of academic research cited in support of supply-side housing policy but argues that important questions remain about the methodologies, assumptions, and real-world applicability of existing studies. Hopkins contends that complex financial, institutional, and market dynamics shape how and why housing gets developed, and that housing researchers have yet to develop a comprehensive theory or methodology to fully explain these forces. The extent to which public policy, as opposed to broader economic conditions and market incentives, constrains housing supply is identified as an area requiring further study. The article positions this not as a rejection of YIMBY goals but as a call for greater intellectual curiosity and less tribal certainty in housing debates. The piece suggests the housing conversation would benefit from fewer fixed ideological positions and more willingness to engage with the limits of current evidence. Rather than treating supply-side deregulation as a settled question, Hopkins argues practitioners and advocates should hold space for the possibility that the relationship between new construction and affordability is more conditional and context-dependent than commonly presented. The article does not dismiss the value of zoning reform or increased housing production. Its core argument is methodological: the field of housing supply research is still developing, and its findings should be treated with appropriate caution rather than deployed as settled doctrine. The subtitle captures the thesis directly: the housing debate needs fewer tribes and more curiosity.

**Why it matters:** Australia's own zoning reform debates, from Victoria's activity centre upzoning to NSW's mid-rise code changes, frequently invoke North American supply-side research as settled evidence. This critique underscores that the evidence base remains contested and context-dependent, warranting caution before transplanting US-derived housing supply assumptions into Australian metropolitan planning.
