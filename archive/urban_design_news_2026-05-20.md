## Urban areas can expand housing supply through transit-oriented development

**Source:** Reason Foundation  
**URL:** https://reason.org/commentary/urban-areas-can-expand-housing-supply-through-transit-oriented-development/  
**Topics:** TOD, Zoning, Housing, US Policy

Published May 12, 2026, this policy analysis argues that while Transit-Oriented Development has historically been framed as a complete solution to urban design problems caused by single-use zoning, utopian expectations have yet to be realized at scale in the United States. Instead, the article proposes implementing TOD through a housing-policy lens to address shortages where transit exists but land-use rules limit supply expansion. It highlights California's recent legislative pairing: SB 79, which takes effect July 1, 2026, classifies transit stops into tiers and establishes minimum density requirements varying by distance from stops, complementing AB 2097's parking mandate elimination. Massachusetts' HB 4977 allows accessory dwelling units by right statewide and prohibits parking requirements for ADUs within one-half mile of transit stops; Massachusetts approved over 1,200 ADUs in 2025 alone. The article also examines Arlington County, Virginia, where the median home price reached $811,245 in February 2026, noting that while TOD creates valuable amenity premiums, restrictive building regulations and Washington DC's height limits push demand into adjacent suburbs, making land scarce and expensive. As of 2026, the Federal Transit Administration has allocated over $68 million annually in competitive grants for TOD planning, yet few places have achieved transformative walkability.

**Why it matters:** These state-level mandates represent a pragmatic pivot from comprehensive place-making to surgical upzoning, offering replicable legislative templates for planners seeking to unlock housing near existing transit without waiting for greenfield rail extensions.

## Highland Park, Texas ceases all transit service

**Source:** Planetizen  
**URL:** https://www.planetizen.com/news  
**Topics:** Transit, Equity, United States, Dallas, Regional Planning

Transit service to Highland Park, Texas, a wealthy enclave entirely surrounded by the city of Dallas, ceased on May 14, 2026. Highland Park, with a population of approximately 9,000 and a median household income exceeding $200,000, is one of the most affluent communities in the Dallas-Fort Worth metropolitan area. The cessation of service marks a rare instance of transit withdrawal from an established urban area and raises questions about transit equity and the relationship between wealthy municipalities and regional transit systems. Highland Park had historically been served by Dallas Area Rapid Transit (DART) routes connecting it to downtown Dallas and other parts of the metropolitan region. The decision appears linked to local governance choices around transit funding participation and land-use policies that have maintained the enclave's low-density, single-family character. Urban planners and transit advocates have expressed concern that the service cessation sets a precedent for opt-out models in other affluent suburban jurisdictions, potentially fragmenting regional transit networks precisely when metropolitan areas are attempting to expand transit-oriented development and reduce car dependency.

**Why it matters:** The withdrawal reveals tensions between municipal autonomy and regional transit planning, highlighting how wealthy enclaves can undermine metropolitan mobility networks and perpetuate spatial inequality through transit opt-outs.

## NSW Building Bill Targets Prefab Homes, Approvals and Certifiers

**Source:** The Urban Developer  
**URL:** https://www.theurbandeveloper.com/articles/nsw-building-bill-prefab-homes-approvals-certifiers-renewable-projects  
**Topics:** Australia, New South Wales, Sydney, Housing, Policy

New South Wales has introduced the Building (Approvals and Practitioners) Bill 2026, tabled in parliament this week, aiming to make NSW the first Australian jurisdiction to formally recognise modern methods of construction (MMC) in law. The Bill would define prefabricated buildings, integrate MMC into the approvals system and introduce consumer protections for prefabricated home buyers. Planning and public spaces minister Paul Scully said the Bill builds on planning reforms passed last year, replacing a fragmented and inconsistent system with a clear and consistent approach. The Commonwealth Productivity Commission estimates MMC, including modular and prefabricated construction, could reduce overall costs by up to 20 per cent and cut build times by 50 per cent compared to traditional methods. Streamlining the fragmented approvals process is another aim, with the government estimating savings of around $330,000 per apartment block by removing duplicative design requirements for identical building elements. Staged approvals would allow construction to begin and occupants to move in earlier. Building minister Anoulack Chanthivong framed the reforms as targeting unnecessary red tape around MMC and prefab housing construction. Conflict-of-interest rules for certifiers would also be tightened, with maximum court-imposed penalties for breaching such rules rising from $33,000 to $1.1 million. Federal housing minister Clare O'Neil released an interim report on National Construction Code reform last month, outlining five priorities including simplifying code access and reducing compliance burdens, noting that each project carries tens of thousands of dollars in compliance costs compounded by around 600 pages of state variations. Prefab and modular manufacturers have in some cases waited up to 10 years to have new products and construction methods recognised in the code. Federal reform proposals are due to go to state and territory building ministers in the second half of 2026.

**Why it matters:** This legislation could significantly accelerate housing delivery across NSW and set a national precedent for streamlining MMC approvals, directly impacting the feasibility and speed of medium- to high-density residential projects while potentially reshaping the construction supply chain for urban infill.
