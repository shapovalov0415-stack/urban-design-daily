## Students branch out and climb trees to learn up close how to care for them and why they help cities flourish

**Source:** The Conversation  
**URL:** https://theconversation.com/students-branch-out-and-climb-trees-to-learn-up-close-how-to-care-for-them-and-why-they-help-cities-flourish-283952  
**Topics:** Australia, Urban Forestry, Landscape Urbanism, Green Infrastructure

An educational program is giving students direct, hands-on experience with urban trees by having them physically climb and inspect specimens as part of their training in arboriculture and urban forest management. The approach combines practical tree care skills with broader lessons about why trees are essential to the functioning and liveability of cities, bridging the gap between technical expertise and an understanding of urban ecological systems at the street and neighbourhood scale. Cities derive substantial environmental benefits from their tree canopy. Trees cool concrete-dominated landscapes through shade and evapotranspiration, reducing surface and ambient temperatures in streets, parks, and public spaces during periods of extreme heat. They also improve air quality by filtering particulate matter and absorbing gaseous pollutants, contributing to better health outcomes for urban populations. These cooling and air quality functions are becoming increasingly critical as cities face more frequent and intense heatwaves driven by climate change, placing urban forestry at the centre of climate adaptation strategy rather than at its margins. Beyond measurable environmental services, urban trees shape the spatial quality and social character of streets and neighbourhoods. Canopy cover enhances pedestrian comfort, encourages walking and cycling, and contributes to the sense of enclosure and human scale that defines walkable, people-oriented streetscapes. Mature urban forests also support biodiversity by providing habitat corridors for birds, insects, and other fauna within otherwise heavily modified environments. The experiential training model described in this program reflects growing recognition that urban forest management requires a dedicated, skilled workforce capable of assessing tree health, managing risk, and guiding species selection. Reported by The Conversation. See linked article for full detail.

**Why it matters:** Urban canopy management is gaining recognition as essential city infrastructure rather than amenity, with experiential training programs building the specialist workforce needed to sustain and expand tree cover. Australian cities pursuing ambitious urban forest strategies face similar workforce pipeline challenges, making the integration of hands-on arboricultural education into green infrastructure planning increasingly relevant.

## In Conversation with Diego Monraz Villaseñor, Secretary of Transport for Jalisco, Mexico

**Source:** ITDP  
**URL:** https://itdp.org/2026/06/23/interview-diego-monraz-villasenor-jalisco-stmagazine-37/  
**Topics:** Mexico, Bus Rapid Transit, Walkable City, Transit-Oriented Development, Public Transport

Guadalajara's state transport secretary Diego Monraz Villaseñor has detailed the evolution of the Mi Macro bus rapid transit system in an interview with the Institute for Transportation and Development Policy. The system now operates two major corridors across Mexico's second-largest metropolitan area, collectively providing more than 475,000 daily trips and forming the backbone of the region's public transport network. The first corridor, Mi Macro Calzada, launched in March 2009 as Jalisco's inaugural BRT line. It runs 16.6 kilometres north-south along Calzada Independencia and Gobernador Curiel Avenue through the municipalities of Guadalajara and Tlaquepaque, serving 27 stations and carrying more than 150,000 passengers daily. A 2024 renovation programme added 26 new bus units, increasing daily capacity by up to 18,000 riders, alongside station upgrades including refreshed signage and automatic platform doors. The second and larger corridor, Mi Macro Periférico, was inaugurated in 2022 along the city's major ring road. Spanning over 49 kilometres with 46 stations, it connects the municipalities of Guadalajara, Zapopan, Tlaquepaque, and Tonalá. In its first three years the line delivered more than 300 million trips, averaging 325,000 per day over the most recent year, making it the largest BRT system in Mexico by length and ridership. Both corridors connect with Lines 1, 2, and 3 of the Mi Tren rail network, with a direct link to Line 4 planned. Across Jalisco, approximately 3.1 million public transport trips occur daily. A statewide fleet renewal of over 2,616 bus units has reduced regional CO2 emissions by 20 percent, equivalent to 23,000 tonnes annually.

**Why it matters:** Guadalajara demonstrates how a mid-income Latin American city can build a high-ridership BRT network that reshapes metropolitan mobility within a single decade, achieving passenger volumes comparable to light rail at lower capital cost. The phased corridor expansion and fleet modernisation strategy offer a transferable model for Australian cities evaluating rapid transit options along orbital routes.

## Foster + Partners Reveals Agricultural City Master Plan in Southern Oman

**Source:** ArchDaily  
**URL:** https://www.archdaily.com/1042758/foster-plus-partners-reveals-agricultural-city-master-plan-in-southern-oman  
**Topics:** Oman, Master Planning, Urban Agriculture, Public Realm, Landscape Urbanism

Foster and Partners, collaborating with Dar Al-Handasah, has revealed the master plan for Al Najd Agricultural City in the Dhofar region of southern Oman. Covering approximately 54 million square feet, the project is conceived as a self-sustaining settlement that integrates agricultural production with urban functions. It was commissioned jointly by Oman's Ministry of Housing and Urban Planning and the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, and Water Resources as part of the national objectives outlined in Oman Vision 2040, which seeks to strengthen food security, diversify the economy, and support sustainable development. The plan is organised around a compact urban centre surrounded by a network of agricultural plots ranging from 10 to 200 hectares. Drawing on historical relationships between agriculture and settlement patterns in Oman, the design extends productive landscapes beyond farmland into parks, public squares, courtyards, and linear green corridors. This multi-scalar approach positions food production as a structuring element of civic and public space rather than a peripheral land use pushed to the urban fringe. The urban core is designed for approximately 13,000 residents and the creation of 6,500 jobs. A network of shaded pedestrian routes connects residential areas with educational, healthcare, cultural, religious, and commercial facilities, while public transportation infrastructure is woven throughout the development. Agricultural production focuses on crops suited to local climatic conditions, including date palms, greenhouse-grown vegetables such as tomatoes, and industrial crops for raw material production. The project also incorporates agro-processing facilities for dried and frozen foods, oils, compost, and fertilisers, intended to support local supply chains while contributing to national food security.

**Why it matters:** The Al Najd master plan tests whether agricultural production can function as primary urban structure rather than being displaced by development, embedding food systems into the public realm at neighbourhood scale. The integration of shaded pedestrian networks with productive landscapes offers a design framework relevant to Australian regional centres navigating heat resilience, food security, and settlement growth.
