## Oil price shocks have exposed car-dependent cities. Here's what governments can do

**Source:** The Conversation  
**URL:** https://theconversation.com/oil-price-shocks-have-exposed-car-dependent-cities-heres-what-governments-can-do-285670  
**Topics:** Canada, Walkable City, Transport Resilience, Car Dependency, Urban Mobility

The recent conflict between Israel, the United States, and Iran triggered a global oil price surge that pushed Canadian gasoline prices past two dollars per litre, exposing deep structural vulnerabilities in car-dependent cities across North America. The International Energy Agency described the resulting supply disruption as the largest in the history of the global oil market, with extensive damage to Persian Gulf oil and gas infrastructure continuing to strain output and a backlog of ships behind the Strait of Hormuz delaying recovery. Canada's exposure is acute. Approximately 80 per cent of commuters rely on private vehicles, 95 per cent of which run on internal combustion engines, driving demand for roughly 43 million cubic metres of gasoline each year. Transportation accounts for a significant portion of household spending, particularly in car-dependent suburbs where alternatives are scarce. When fuel prices spike, those households absorb the cost with few options to adjust. The article frames this not as an isolated energy event but as a systemic urban planning failure. Cities designed around a single mode of transport are inherently fragile, whether the disruption comes from fuel price shocks, climate-induced extreme weather, or future pandemics. Resilient urban mobility requires giving people genuine alternatives including walking, cycling, public transit, and electric vehicles. Practical measures include mandating electric vehicle charging infrastructure in new developments, investing in protected cycling networks, and expanding public transit coverage. The analysis argues that cities which diversify their transport systems reduce both the energy intensity of movement and the economic shock transmitted to households when any single mode is disrupted.

**Why it matters:** Car-dependent urban forms create compounding household vulnerability during energy supply shocks, a pattern directly relevant to Australian outer suburbs where private vehicle reliance mirrors Canadian levels. The case strengthens arguments for multimodal transport investment and walkable neighbourhood planning as economic resilience measures, not just amenity improvements.

## The Ecological Intelligence of Sacred Landscapes

**Source:** ArchDaily  
**URL:** https://www.archdaily.com/1042553/the-ecological-intelligence-of-sacred-landscapes  
**Topics:** India, Landscape Urbanism, Water-Sensitive Urban Design, Green Infrastructure, Urban Ecology

Contemporary landscape architecture frequently treats ecological design as a recent innovation, positioning biodiversity corridors, sponge cities, and regenerative landscapes as emerging responses to environmental crisis. A detailed essay on ArchDaily by Ananya Nayak challenges this framing by examining centuries-old sacred landscapes across India and the SWANA region that have long performed precisely these ecological functions through cultural and spiritual practice rather than explicit environmental agendas. The article centres on temple tanks, known as pushkarani, found across South India. These excavated reservoirs sit beside temple complexes and serve simultaneously ritual, spatial, and environmental roles. They collect seasonal rainfall, support groundwater recharge, moderate local microclimates, and provide water for everyday use. Historically, many tanks were not isolated features but components of larger watershed systems connecting settlements to surrounding agricultural landscapes. During drought they functioned as reserves, and during heavy rainfall they absorbed and retained excess water. In cities such as Madurai, Kanchipuram, and Thanjavur, urban life organized itself around the tank as environmental infrastructure. Sacred groves similarly protected biodiversity through cultural practice rather than explicit conservation policy, while oasis settlements in arid SWANA regions sustained communities by managing scarce water resources through spatial and social systems. The essay argues that many conditions now discussed through more-than-human design frameworks have existed for centuries within landscapes that architects rarely study as ecological infrastructure. These precedents offer practical models for contemporary water-sensitive urban design and stormwater management, suggesting that effective green infrastructure can draw on deeply embedded cultural-spatial systems rather than being invented from first principles.

**Why it matters:** Centuries-old water management systems in Indian temple tanks demonstrate that effective green infrastructure does not require novel engineering but can build on embedded cultural-spatial precedents. For Australian water-sensitive urban design practice, this reframes stormwater and urban ecology as design traditions to learn from rather than technical problems to solve from scratch.

## New bus rapid transit line opens in Salt Lake City

**Source:** Smart Cities Dive  
**URL:** https://www.smartcitiesdive.com/news/utah-transit-authority-brt-streetcar-construction/823831/  
**Topics:** United States, Bus Rapid Transit, Active Transport, Transit-Oriented Development, Multimodal Mobility

The Utah Transit Authority opened the all-electric Midvalley Express bus rapid transit line in Salt Lake City, the latest in a significant wave of fixed-guideway transit projects opening across North America in 2026. The new BRT service connects local destinations and planned developments with the transit hub on the Taylorsville campus of Salt Lake Community College, forming part of a broader strategy to expand multimodal access along the Wasatch Front as Utah's population continues to grow. The opening sits within a landmark year for North American transit expansion. Approximately 150 miles of new fixed-guideway lines are expected to open across the continent in 2026, with 13 projects in the United States alone. Los Angeles has extended one of its subway lines by nearly four miles. Seattle completed the final seven-mile segment of its light rail line across Lake Washington. Kansas City extended its streetcar to the riverfront, and Atlanta opened that metropolitan area's first bus rapid transit line. Bus ridership nationally is approaching pre-pandemic levels, providing a favourable demand context for investment in new services. The Salt Lake expansion also builds on the existing two-mile S-Line streetcar, which has operated since 2013 alongside a dedicated bike and pedestrian greenway linking residential and commercial areas. The S-Line connects with the TRAX light rail system and transit bus network, and an extension is expected to reach completion by fall 2027. UTA Executive Director Jay Fox noted the agency's focus on expanding access, improving frequency, and investing in modern infrastructure to serve growing demand across the region.

**Why it matters:** North America's 2026 transit expansion wave, with 150 miles of new fixed-guideway lines across 13 US projects, demonstrates sustained municipal commitment to multimodal networks despite federal funding uncertainty. Salt Lake City's integration of BRT with an existing streetcar greenway corridor offers a replicable model for Australian cities connecting rapid transit investment with active transport infrastructure.
