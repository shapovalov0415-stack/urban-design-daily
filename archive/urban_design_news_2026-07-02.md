## By 2050, many Sydney apartments built to today's standards could be too hot for weeks at a time

**Source:** The Fifth Estate  
**URL:** https://thefifthestate.com.au/the-conversation/by-2050-many-sydney-apartments-built-to-todays-standards-could-be-too-hot-for-weeks-at-a-time/  
**Topics:** Australia, Sydney, Climate Resilience, Building Standards, Urban Density

Research published in the journal Energy and Buildings has found that Sydney apartments built to current Australian building standards could experience sustained indoor temperatures above 30 degrees Celsius for days at a time by 2050. The study modelled how mid-century climate projections interact with the thermal performance requirements of the current National Construction Code, concluding that compliance with today's standards will not guarantee liveable conditions under future heat scenarios. The findings carry particular weight for Western Sydney, where Penrith recorded 48.9 degrees in January 2020, the highest temperature ever measured in Greater Sydney. By 2050, the researchers project that extreme heat events will last longer and occur more frequently, with compounding risks when electricity grid failures coincide with peak demand days. Sydney is currently the nation's leader in apartment living and is undergoing rapid rezoning to accommodate population growth and meet state housing targets. The volume of new multi-unit construction planned across the metropolitan area means that design and performance decisions made today will lock in thermal outcomes for decades. If current energy efficiency provisions prove inadequate under warming conditions, a large share of the city's future housing stock could require costly retrofit or mechanical cooling upgrades that strain household budgets and grid capacity alike. The research raises questions about whether the National Construction Code's energy efficiency provisions should incorporate forward-looking climate data rather than relying on historical weather files. Several state governments are reviewing residential performance standards, and the study provides quantitative evidence that current benchmarks may already be outdated for the conditions buildings will face during their operational lifetimes.

**Why it matters:** Forward-looking climate modelling applied to current building codes reveals a measurable gap between regulatory intent and real-world performance under mid-century conditions. With Sydney rezoning at scale and other Australian capitals following similar densification paths, the findings strengthen the case for embedding climate projection data into the National Construction Code's energy efficiency provisions.

## The D.C. Office of Planning releases Gensler's masterplan for Robert F. Kennedy Campus

**Source:** Architects Newspaper  
**URL:** https://www.archpaper.com/2026/07/masterplan-robert-f-kennedy-campus/  
**Topics:** United States, Public Realm, Mixed-Use Development, Landscape Urbanism, Urban Design

The District of Columbia Office of Planning has released a master plan for the 180-acre Robert F. Kennedy Campus on the Anacostia River, setting out an urban design framework for one of the largest mixed-use redevelopments currently proposed in the United States capital. Prepared by Gensler for the city-owned land and Perkins Eastman for the portions under team control, the plan covers the site of the demolished RFK Memorial Stadium and is coordinated with a stadium design by HKS and landscape plans by OJB. The framework calls for between 5,500 and 6,500 new housing units, with 30 percent designated as affordable. Thirty percent of the total site area is allocated to public space and greenery. The new stadium will rise to a maximum of 130 feet, with surrounding development blocks capped at 110 feet, consistent with the District's 1910 Height of Buildings Act. The plan also envisions community-serving retail, neighbourhood services and entertainment destinations distributed across the campus. Six distinct districts organise the site: the stadium, plaza, riverfront and recreation districts, plus Kingman and Anacostia Commons. Anacostia Commons will establish a 200-foot building-free buffer along the riverbank, creating what the plan describes as a natural riparian zone connecting the campus to the water's edge. The Office of Planning reported that 33 community engagement events drew input from approximately 1,800 participants during the planning process. Renderings depict brick-and-glass-faced buildings, activated streetscapes, and walking and cycling trails leading toward the riverfront, signalling an intent to integrate the campus into the broader urban fabric rather than isolating it as a single-purpose sports venue.

**Why it matters:** A 180-acre mixed-use master plan anchored by a sports stadium but dedicating 30 percent of site area to public space and greenery demonstrates how cities can leverage major venue investment to deliver housing, riverfront access and activated streetscapes. The 200-foot riparian buffer and height controls offer a replicable model for balancing density with public realm quality.

## The Obama Presidential Center: "It's a Canvas. People Are Leaving Their Imprint on It"

**Source:** Common Edge  
**URL:** https://commonedge.org/the-obama-presidential-center-its-a-canvas-people-are-leaving-their-imprint-on-it/  
**Topics:** United States, Placemaking, Public Realm, Landscape Architecture

The Obama Presidential Center opened to the public on Juneteenth 2026, occupying approximately 19 acres in Jackson Park on Chicago's South Side. The 850-million-dollar privately funded campus includes a museum, a branch of the Chicago Public Library, 30 newly commissioned artworks, an athletic facility, and a series of outdoor spaces that architecture critic Lee Bey of the Chicago Sun-Times has described as potentially the centre's most significant legacy. Tod Williams Billie Tsien Architects designed the main building, Michael Van Valkenburgh Associates handled the landscape architecture, and Moody Nolan, one of the largest African American-owned design firms in the United States, designed the athletic facility. Critical reaction to the centre has been divided, with much attention focused on a 225-foot tower that the Obama Foundation said was intended to evoke four upraised hands. Bey, a South Side resident who initially opposed the tower's form, has argued that visiting the completed campus in its neighbourhood context reveals a different reading than photographs or renderings suggest. He noted that the outdoor spaces are functioning as a kind of public canvas, with visitors using the grounds in ways that generate social activity and community ownership of the site. Early criticism also addressed the foundation's decision not to enter a formal community benefits agreement, raising questions about public engagement during the planning process. Bey contends that the completed landscape and public realm are now demonstrating their value through observed use patterns rather than formal agreements, with South Side residents claiming the space on their own terms. The project's integration of cultural programming, library services and open space across a single campus reflects a model of institutional placemaking that extends beyond the museum building itself.

**Why it matters:** Chicago's Obama Presidential Center demonstrates how institutional campuses can function as public realm infrastructure when landscape and open space are treated as primary design elements rather than leftover areas. The project's emphasis on outdoor social activity and community use patterns over formal built form echoes soft-city principles of designing for street-level human engagement.
