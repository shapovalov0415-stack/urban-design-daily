## Five Years After Chicago's Equitable TOD Policy: Progress Report Shows Foundation for Affordability and Connectivity

**Source:** Streetsblog Chicago  
**URL:** https://chi.streetsblog.org/2026/05/19/five-years-after-city-of-chicago-signed-off-on-a-policy-plan-the-state-of-etod-in-chicago-report-notes-progress-creating-transit-friendly-affordable-housing  
**Topics:** Chicago, Transit-Oriented Development, Equity, Housing, Public Transit

Five years after Chicago adopted its first Equitable Transit Oriented Development (ETOD) policy plan in 2021, a coalition of city planners and advocates released a comprehensive progress report on 19 May 2026. The plan was developed by Elevated Chicago and the Mayor's Office in response to TOD projects between 2013 and 2015 that disproportionately benefited North Side neighborhoods and accelerated displacement of longtime residents. The report documents tangible gains: transit culture projects have been awarded to community organizations including Mandala South Asian Performing Arts, Endeleo Institute, and the McKinley Park Development Council in 2026. A notable example is the Green Line Performing Arts Center next to the 53rd Green Line Station in Washington Park. Outreach efforts have included walking tours for elected officials, engagement in schools and universities, and educational materials on right-sizing parking. The Center for Neighborhood Technology and Metropolitan Planning Council created the ETOD Social Impact Calculator to help developers measure the value of TOD. The report concludes with recommendations for Chicago's newly transitioning transit authority (NITA), urging it to hire dedicated ETOD staff, partner across sectors, invest immediately in ETOD projects, and provide technical assistance to community developers.

**Why it matters:** Chicago's five-year retrospective demonstrates how embedding equity frameworks into TOD policy from the outset can shift outcomes toward affordability and community stability, offering a replicable template for cities seeking to avoid gentrification driven by transit investment.

## Urban Green Spaces Curb Climate Risk but Widen Housing Inequality: KAIST Study on African Cities

**Source:** Seoul Economic Daily  
**URL:** https://en.sedaily.com/technology/2026/05/18/urban-green-spaces-curb-climate-risk-but-widen-housing  
**Topics:** Climate Adaptation, Gentrification, Public Space, Housing Inequality, Africa

A study published in Nature Cities on 13 April 2026 by researchers from KAIST, Peking University, and NYU Shanghai analyzed 221 cities across 32 African countries and found that 'green-blue adaptation' policies—expanding urban parks, green spaces, and water features to combat heatwaves and flooding—can simultaneously trigger gentrification and displace original residents. The research team, led by Professor Kim Seung-kyum of KAIST's Department of AI and Future Studies, combined satellite imagery and socioeconomic data from 503 areas within 221 metropolitan zones between 2005 and 2024. Areas with climate adaptation facilities recorded an average 41 per cent rise in a composite gentrification index covering housing prices, population inflow, and neighborhood change, compared with areas without such facilities. Housing prices climbed approximately 13 per cent, and inflows of outside residents rose significantly. The researchers concluded that facilities created to protect citizens from the climate crisis paradoxically deepened housing insecurity for economically vulnerable groups. The team recommended viewing climate adaptation not simply as infrastructure but as a 'distribution issue' requiring simultaneous housing stability measures including protection of land ownership rights, public housing supply, and anti-displacement policies.

**Why it matters:** This continent-wide empirical analysis quantifies the causal link between climate adaptation infrastructure and gentrification, underscoring the imperative for urban designers to integrate housing affordability and tenure protection into climate resilience strategies from project inception.

## Making it easier to build homes in NSW

**Source:** NSW Government  
**URL:** https://www.nsw.gov.au/ministerial-releases/making-it-easier-to-build-homes-nsw  
**Topics:** Australia, NSW, Housing, Policy, Approvals

The Minns Labor Government announced on 14 May 2026 two new approval pathways that could halve assessment times for low-rise homes, which account for 70 per cent of all development applications in New South Wales. Planning Minister Paul Scully unveiled a targeted assessment pathway for low-rise housing and an expanded complying development process that allows minor variations to standards while maintaining the 20-day fast-track approval timeframe. Currently, proposals that fail to meet even a single development standard for complying development must go through the full development application process, adding more than 60 days and significant paperwork to the assessment. The new variation pathway will allow straightforward proposals with minor departures from standards to use the faster complying development route. A separate targeted assessment pathway is proposed specifically for low-rise homes, aiming to reduce typical 80-day DA assessment times by half. Both proposals are on public exhibition on the NSW Planning Portal until 24 June 2026, with feedback informing staged implementation. The reforms build on the state's existing Low and Mid-Rise Housing Policy and NSW Pattern Book initiatives, which have already expanded permissions for duplexes, terraces, townhouses and apartments within 800 metres of 171 nominated town centres and transport hubs across metropolitan Sydney, Central Coast, Illawarra-Shoalhaven and Hunter regions. The government argues the changes will free councils to focus resources on larger or more complex planning matters while speeding delivery of homes needed to meet National Housing Accord targets of 1.2 million dwellings over five years.

**Why it matters:** These process reforms complement NSW's aggressive spatial rezoning programme and signal a dual-track strategy—liberalising both what can be built and how quickly approvals flow—with direct implications for urban form, density distribution, and the feasibility of infill housing at neighbourhood scale.
