## 2026–27 ACT Budget puts housing first: stamp duty axed for first home buyers, missing-middle pattern book launched
**Source:** ACT Government  **URL:** https://www.act.gov.au/our-canberra/latest-news/2026/june/202627-act-budget-more-homes-for-those-who-need-it-most  **Topics:** Australia, Canberra, ACT, Housing, Planning Reform

The 2026–27 ACT Budget, handed down by Chief Minister and Treasurer Andrew Barr on 5 June 2026, frames housing as its central commitment and makes Canberra the first Australian jurisdiction to abolish stamp duty for all first home buyers from 1 July 2026. On the supply side, the budget funds land releases to support close to 26,000 new homes across the Territory over five years, part of a broader pledge to enable 30,000 new homes by the end of 2030. The centrepiece for urban form is a new Canberra House Pattern Book, a collection of pre-approved 'missing middle' designs such as terraces, duplexes and small apartment buildings that can proceed without a development application, paired with a package of tax cuts aimed at making missing-middle delivery financially viable. For social and affordable housing, the Government will build and buy an extra 450 public homes through a new Public Housing Pipeline program, with more than half supported by the Commonwealth's Housing Australia Future Fund Facility Round 3, and progress more than 1,000 affordable homes via a loan guarantee program. Longer-term targets include lifting the public housing portfolio to 13,200 dwellings and delivering 5,000 additional public, community and affordable rental homes by 2030, alongside 20-year indexed funding for crisis and transitional housing in partnership with the Commonwealth and new money to repair and maintain ageing stock. Together the measures pair demand-side relief with planning reform and direct public investment.

**Why it matters:** The pattern-book-plus-tax-cut model shows how a city can pre-approve gentle density at scale, turning 'missing middle' from a slogan into a by-right delivery pathway that urban designers elsewhere can study and adapt.

## Which US cities have the most housing near transit?
**Source:** Planetizen  **URL:** https://www.planetizen.com/news/2026/06/137761-which-us-cities-have-most-housing-near-transit  **Topics:** United States, Transit-Oriented Development, Housing, Transit, Equity

Planetizen reports on a new interactive analysis from the Urban Institute, led by researcher Yonah Freemark, mapping which US metropolitan regions have actually succeeded in building housing near public transport after four decades of transit-oriented development (TOD) advocacy. The tool quantifies both the supply of bus and rail service and how much housing sits within walking distance of it, exposing stark regional inequality: residents of the New York, San Francisco and Washington, DC regions enjoy more than four times the per-capita bus and rail service of residents in car-dependent metros such as Detroit, Indianapolis and Memphis. The analysis underscores that TOD outcomes track closely with long-run transit investment and supportive land-use rules. Regions that paired frequent service with zoning allowing density near stations have concentrated far more homes within reach of transit, while those that built rail but left surrounding parcels zoned for low density captured little of the potential ridership or affordability benefit. Freemark frames TOD not as a single project type but as a systems outcome: the payoff of decades of coordinated capital spending, operations funding and parcel-level upzoning. The piece lands amid intensifying federal pressure on transit budgets, making the question of where housing and service actually coincide newly urgent for agencies defending their networks. For planners, the interactive offers a benchmarking lens, a way to see whether their region is converting transit dollars into transit-accessible homes, or simply running trains past neighborhoods that remain off-limits to the residents who would ride them.

**Why it matters:** It reframes TOD as a measurable regional outcome rather than a project label, giving urban designers a data-driven yardstick for whether transit investment is actually being matched by the zoning that lets people live next to it.

## California city permanently bans data centers by ballot measure
**Source:** Planetizen  **URL:** https://www.planetizen.com/news/2026/06/137739-california-city-permanently-bans-data-centers  **Topics:** United States, Data Centers, Land Use, Referendum, Environment

Voters in Monterey Park, California, a small city in the San Gabriel Valley just east of Los Angeles, approved a measure permanently banning data centers from the municipality, with roughly 86 percent in favour in a June 2026 special election. As reported by Planetizen, it is the first US city to enact such a prohibition directly through a ballot measure, with the ban remaining in force unless voters themselves move to reverse it, locking the decision into the city's land-use framework beyond the reach of a single council vote. Proponents framed the prohibition as a way to protect local air quality, drinking-water resources and public health, and to shield residents from the electricity- and water-rate pressures that large-scale computing facilities can impose on a community's utilities. The vote was the culmination of escalating local opposition to a specific proposal to convert a vacant office complex into a data center; the city council had already passed a 45-day moratorium in January 2026, followed by a 10-month extension in March, before residents moved to make the restriction permanent. The result is part of a wider 2026 wave of municipal and state pushback against data-center development, including referendums and moratoria in Wisconsin, Maine and New York, as the land, power and water demands of AI-era computing collide with local planning authority and community priorities.

**Why it matters:** Data centers are becoming a frontline land-use conflict, and Monterey Park shows residents using direct democracy to write computing infrastructure out of the zoning code, a precedent urban designers will need to weigh as energy-hungry facilities compete with housing for scarce serviced land.
