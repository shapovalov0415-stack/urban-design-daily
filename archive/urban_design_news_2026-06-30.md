## Revealing the human side of why some people need social housing

**Source:** The Fifth Estate  
**URL:** https://thefifthestate.com.au/columns/spinifex/revealing-the-human-side-of-why-some-people-need-social-housing/  
**Topics:** Australia, New South Wales, Social Housing, Housing Policy, Urban Inequality

Australia's social housing crisis continues to deepen despite increased government investment, with New South Wales data revealing a waiting list that has grown substantially over the past year. The NSW Housing Register recorded 70,257 applicant households in May 2026, up from 66,698 in May 2025, representing a net increase of more than 3,500 households. The priority list, reserved for those in greatest need, rose from 11,411 to 13,293 over the same period. Alan Morris, writing in The Fifth Estate, argues that the raw numbers obscure the human reality of prolonged housing insecurity. Applicants who are not on the priority list face wait times that can stretch for years, with location preferences further constraining their prospects. The allocation zone system means that choosing a specific area such as Mount Druitt can dramatically affect how long a household waits for an offer. Federal and state governments have acknowledged the scale of the deficit and committed to new construction pipelines, yet the rate of completions and the concurrent demolition of ageing stock mean the net gain remains insufficient to absorb demand. The growing gap between supply commitments and waitlist growth raises questions about whether current policy settings can deliver meaningful reduction in housing stress within this decade. For urban designers and planners, the data underscores the importance of integrating social and affordable housing into broader precinct planning rather than treating it as a residual land use. Mixed-tenure developments in well-serviced locations remain the most effective way to reduce spatial inequality while supporting the walkable, mixed-use neighbourhoods that contemporary planning frameworks promote.

**Why it matters:** Growing waitlist data from NSW demonstrates that construction pipelines alone cannot resolve social housing shortages without concurrent reforms to planning settings, mixed-tenure requirements, and the protection of existing stock from demolition. State governments face mounting pressure to embed affordable housing mandates within precinct-level rezoning frameworks.

## 'It's open green space versus construction': Delhi's historic polo club and the battle for the city's future

**Source:** The Guardian  
**URL:** https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/jun/29/park-strife-delhis-historic-polo-club-becomes-a-battlefield-for-the-citys-fate  
**Topics:** India, Urban Green Space, Public Realm, Climate Adaptation, Landscape Urbanism

The Indian government's requisition of the Jaipur Polo Ground in central Delhi has triggered a fierce public debate about the future of urban green space in one of the world's most heat-stressed capitals. The six-hectare site, given to the Delhi Polo Club by the Maharaja of Jaipur nearly a century ago, was taken over by government officials this week, with gates padlocked and an era of recreational use brought to an abrupt end. The controversy extends beyond the polo ground itself. The nearby Delhi Gymkhana Club, spread across eleven hectares, faces a similar takeover threat. Both cases remain before the courts, but the government's intent to redevelop the sites has alarmed environmentalists, residents, and members of the judiciary. High court judge Neena Bansal Krishna captured the prevailing sentiment, warning that the city's residents would suffocate if the government removed one of their few remaining green lungs. Delhi routinely experiences extreme summer temperatures and its air quality ranks among the worst of any major global city. Open green spaces in the urban core serve critical ecological functions, moderating the heat island effect, filtering particulate matter, and providing accessible respite for millions of residents. The loss of even modest green parcels in a city already deficient in per-capita open space carries measurable consequences for public health and thermal comfort. The dispute illustrates the tension that arises when governments view centrally located open land primarily as a development asset rather than as essential urban infrastructure serving cooling, recreation, and ecological functions that underpin liveability.

**Why it matters:** Delhi's green-space dispute demonstrates how rapidly cities lose critical cooling and air-quality infrastructure when open land is treated as a development reserve rather than essential urban fabric. Australian cities managing infill development in warming climates face analogous trade-offs between density targets and the retention of permeable, vegetated ground in established areas.

## The Costs They Don't Count

**Source:** Strong Towns  
**URL:** https://www.strongtowns.org/journal/2026-6-25-the-costs-they-dont-count  
**Topics:** United States, Street Safety, Walkable Urbanism, Transport Planning, Complete Streets

A Strong Towns analysis by Andy Boenau challenges the cost-benefit frameworks that transportation agencies use to justify major road-expansion projects, arguing that conventional models systematically exclude the human and economic toll of traffic fatalities and serious injuries. Approximately one hundred people are killed in car crashes across the United States every day, a figure that represents not only devastating personal loss but also a significant and largely unaccounted economic cost. Boenau contends that transportation professionals routinely approve projects that increase vehicle-miles travelled and time spent in traffic, both of which correlate directly with higher crash risk. The analysis highlights a structural flaw in how agencies assign value to human life when evaluating infrastructure investments. While federal guidance provides a statistical value of a human life for use in benefit-cost calculations, the figure is applied inconsistently and is frequently overshadowed by projected travel-time savings and congestion-relief metrics that favour road widening over safer multimodal alternatives. The piece argues that if the true costs of traffic violence were factored into project appraisals with the same rigour as construction and maintenance expenditures, many road-expansion schemes would fail to demonstrate a positive return. Safer alternatives, including narrower streets, protected cycling infrastructure, and investment in walkable land-use patterns, would score more competitively under a framework that accounts for avoided fatalities and reduced healthcare and emergency-services burden. The argument reinforces the case for complete-streets policies that prioritise human-scale movement over vehicle throughput, treating safety as a non-negotiable design input rather than an externality.

**Why it matters:** Conventional transport cost-benefit analysis that excludes crash fatalities and injury costs systematically biases investment toward road expansion over safer, walkable alternatives. Australian road agencies evaluating motorway and arterial projects could adopt full-cost accounting to better justify investment in complete streets and active-transport infrastructure.
