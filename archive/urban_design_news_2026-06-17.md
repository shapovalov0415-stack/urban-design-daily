## Northwest Utilities’ Wildfire Risk—and Spending—Is Skyrocketing

**Source:** Sightline Institute  
**URL:** https://www.sightline.org/2026/06/16/northwest-utilities-wildfire-risk-and-spending-is-skyrocketing/  
**Topics:** Transit-Oriented Development

Next City reports that the largest investor-owned utilities serving the US Pacific Northwest are confronting a rapid escalation in wildfire risk that is reshaping their capital plans. Avista, Idaho Power, Portland General Electric, PacifiCorp and Puget Sound Energy now collectively forecast around $7 billion in wildfire-mitigation spending over the next decade — replacing wood poles with steel and composite, undergrounding lines in the highest-risk corridors, deploying high-resolution weather stations, and adopting public-safety power shutoffs during extreme fire weather. Recovery costs from the 2020 Labor Day fires in Oregon and the 2023 Maui blaze have made underwriters more reluctant to cover utility liability, pushing risk back onto ratepayers and prompting regulatory fights over how much of that bill belongs on customer accounts. The reporting frames the spending as a structural shift: distribution networks built last century for an era of less-volatile weather now have to be re-engineered for compounding climate hazard, with consequences for rate structures, asset siting, and the political economy of who pays for utility resilience in fire-prone landscapes.

**Why it matters:** Wildfire-driven utility hardening will increasingly drive land-use decisions in the wildland-urban interface — siting policy, easement widths, defensible-space rules, and the suburban edge itself — so urban designers need to track how grid resilience translates into where homes can and cannot be approved.

## Yes, a Land Value Tax Is Possible in Washington State

**Source:** Sightline Institute  
**URL:** https://www.sightline.org/2026/06/03/yes-a-land-value-tax-is-possible-in-washington-state/  
**Topics:** Urban Design

Next City surveys a renewed Washington State campaign to authorise a split-rate land value tax (LVT), arguing the policy is more politically achievable than commonly assumed. The piece explains the mechanics — taxing the unimproved land at a higher rate than buildings, which penalises speculative landbanking and rewards investment in housing or commercial improvements — and walks through the constitutional and statutory hurdles Washington faces, including its uniformity clause and the limits of property-tax classification. Drawing on Pennsylvania's split-rate experience and recent academic modelling, the article suggests Washington could pilot the approach via charter-city authority or as a state-enabled local option, particularly in transit-accessible neighbourhoods where holding costs currently allow underused parcels to sit dormant beside high-demand rail and frequent bus corridors. Coalition possibilities are discussed: pro-housing reformers, fiscal conservatives concerned about regressive sales-tax reliance, and progressive groups focused on speculator taxation all surface as potential supporters. The piece lands as Seattle, Tacoma and several Snohomish County councils debate upzoning around new light-rail extensions, making the speculation-on-vacant-land problem newly visible to elected officials.

**Why it matters:** If even one major US state moves on a split-rate LVT, it would be the first significant attempt in decades to tackle land speculation through the tax code rather than through planning regulation — a tool urban designers should understand because it directly affects whether upzoned parcels actually get built on.

## Townhouse boom: Victoria's planning reforms drive 50% surge in low-rise housing applications

**Source:** Premier of Victoria  
**URL:** https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/townhouse-boom-after-labor-planning-reforms  
**Topics:** Australia, Melbourne, Victoria, Planning Reform, Housing

Fresh figures released by the Allan Labor Government show that Victoria's Townhouse and Low-Rise Code, introduced in March 2025, has triggered a near-50 percent jump in planning applications for duplexes, townhouses and three-storey low-rise apartments. Applications across the state grew from about 4,800 homes in the year before the code took effect to roughly 7,000 in the year after, with the strongest response coming from Melbourne's established middle-ring suburbs where permits climbed from 2,000 to more than 3,100 over the same period. Among metropolitan councils, Monash led the surge with 515 homes, followed by Merri-bek with 452, Banyule with 402, and Darebin with 345 — corridor councils long flagged for upzoning capacity around train and tram lines. The code provides a deemed-to-comply pathway: projects that meet a defined set of setback, height, tree-canopy, solar-access and street-amenity standards proceed without a discretionary planning permit, removing months of assessment time and the consultant fees that come with it. The numbers land as Victoria pursues its share of the National Housing Accord's 1.2 million-homes target and as the state government rolls out adjacent reforms — Activity Centre planning, an overhaul of Rescode, and Plan for Victoria — all aimed at lifting medium-density supply in suburbs traditionally hostile to anything taller than a single house.

**Why it matters:** The early Townhouse Code numbers are the strongest evidence yet that a well-drafted deemed-to-comply pathway can shift gentle-density supply at scale, and the suburb-level breakdown gives urban designers a concrete template for which middle-ring catchments respond fastest when the planning friction is removed.
