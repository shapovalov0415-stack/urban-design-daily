## Why are we still preselling apartments rather than embracing a 'build then sell' model?

**Source:** The Fifth Estate  
**URL:** https://thefifthestate.com.au/columns/spinifex/why-are-we-still-preselling-apartments-rather-than-embracing-a-build-then-sell-model/  
**Topics:** Australia, Housing Supply, Apartment Development, Urban Density

Andrea Sharam argues that Australia's dominant apartment presale model produces suboptimal design outcomes and contributes to housing supply instability, proposing a shift toward a build-then-sell approach used in several international markets. Under the presale system, developers must secure a threshold of off-the-plan purchases before construction finance is released, creating a dependency on market sentiment that distorts decision-making throughout the development pipeline. During the design phase, developers trade off construction efficiency against the risk of losing equity if demand softens, resulting in compromises to apartment quality, layout, and amenity provision. The build-then-sell alternative would allow developers to complete construction before marketing individual units, removing the speculative pressure that currently shapes design briefs. Sharam contends this model would deliver several structural benefits: additional working capital for builders during construction, higher-quality housing outcomes driven by end-user expectations rather than investor appeal, more stable demand cycles less prone to boom-bust volatility, and improved workplace conditions that support skilled labour retention in the construction sector. The analysis builds on a previous article examining how procurement of design services under the presale model leads to less than optimal outcomes, with developers prioritising cost containment over design excellence. The argument carries particular weight given Australia's ongoing housing supply challenges, where apartment feasibility remains constrained by construction costs, interest rates, and planning uncertainty. A shift in development financing models could reshape incentive structures across the entire supply chain, aligning builder, designer, and purchaser interests more closely toward delivering well-designed medium-density housing at scale. Reported by The Fifth Estate. See linked article for full detail.

**Why it matters:** Apartment development models directly shape housing quality and density outcomes, and Australia's presale system creates incentive structures that work against the well-designed, human-scale infill housing that walkable urbanism requires. A financing model shift could better align developer, builder, and designer interests toward medium-density housing that supports liveable streetscapes.

## Dreaming in the Ruins: How a Sleeping Ritual in Logroño Proposes a New Civic Architecture

**Source:** ArchDaily  
**URL:** https://www.archdaily.com/1042854/dreaming-in-the-ruins-how-a-sleeping-ritual-in-logrono-proposes-a-new-civic-architecture  
**Topics:** Spain, Public Realm, Urban Installation, Placemaking, Civic Architecture

The Concéntrico 2026 architecture and design festival in Logroño, Spain, featured an installation titled One Third of Life by Finnish-Italian collective Suomi/Koivisto and IC-98 that used a communal sleeping ritual to challenge conventional approaches to urban resilience and civic engagement. The work invited participants to sleep together in the ruins of a historic site, proposing that collective rest could function as a form of civic architecture capable of addressing dimensions of urban experience that data-driven planning tools typically overlook. The installation responded to a broader critique of how contemporary cities approach resilience planning. While urban management increasingly relies on data collection across climate, infrastructure, biodiversity, and social fragmentation indicators, the emotional and imaginative dimensions of urban transformation are rarely treated as legitimate planning inputs. The designers drew on philosopher Felix Guattari's framework of three ecologies, which holds that meaningful ecological transformation requires simultaneous attention to the ecology of the mind, society, and the environment, rather than isolating any single domain as a technical problem to be solved. By situating a sleeping ritual within an urban festival context, the project argued that ancient communal practices engaging body, community, and environment simultaneously offer a model for addressing the polarisation, civic disengagement, and ecological breakdown that characterise many contemporary cities. The work positioned sensory and embodied experience as essential components of public realm design, suggesting that urban interventions need not be permanent infrastructure to generate lasting civic value. The installation sat within Concéntrico's broader program of temporary urban interventions distributed across Logroño's historic centre, a festival format that treats the city itself as both gallery and laboratory for experimental approaches to public space.

**Why it matters:** Logroño's sleeping installation demonstrates that public realm interventions need not be permanent infrastructure to generate civic value, challenging the assumption that urban resilience must be delivered exclusively through technical systems. Australian placemaking programs could draw on this integration of sensory experience and temporary intervention in heritage settings to broaden community engagement methods.

## New Orleans, Like Every City, Is Mortal

**Source:** Common Edge  
**URL:** https://commonedge.org/new-orleans-like-every-city-is-mortal/  
**Topics:** United States, Climate Adaptation, Managed Retreat, Coastal Resilience, Urban Planning

A peer-reviewed study published in Nature Sustainability has delivered a stark assessment of New Orleans' long-term viability, concluding that coastal Louisiana has evidently already crossed the point of no return in its battle against sea-level rise and land subsidence. The multi-institutional research team, which included scholars from Tulane University, analysed the effects of sea-level rise on the Gulf Coast and projected that the region's coastline will migrate further inland by as much as 62 miles. By 2070, the study anticipates that 75 per cent of the region's remaining coastal wetlands will be lost, with the Gulf of Mexico potentially surrounding the city before the century's end. Louisiana has already lost approximately 2,000 square miles of land, an area equivalent to the state of Delaware, to coastal erosion over the past century. The researchers recommend that relocation planning begin immediately, with a managed transition northward over several generations. Writing in response, a New Orleans-based urbanist argues that accepting the city's mortality need not constitute surrender but rather a catalyst for the decisive action required to prevent the worst outcomes. The essay, originally published in The Lens, contends that New Orleans must move beyond the myths it has constructed about its permanence and confront entrenched racial inequalities masked by the city's public brand of celebration and resilience. The study positions New Orleans alongside other low-lying coastal cities including Miami, Norfolk, and Charleston as facing existential climate threats that demand fundamentally new approaches to long-range urban planning. More than two decades after Hurricane Katrina flooded 80 per cent of the city, the findings frame managed retreat not as an admission of failure but as the most responsible planning response to irreversible environmental change.

**Why it matters:** The Nature Sustainability findings on coastal Louisiana establish a benchmark for managed retreat planning that Australian coastal cities, particularly low-lying areas facing compounding flood and erosion risk, will increasingly need to reference as climate adaptation moves from theoretical framework to operational urban planning necessity.
